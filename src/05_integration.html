Multi-omics · biology

How the layers talk to each other

Before integrating data computationally, decide what biological relationship you expect between the layers. The relationship determines which features you can link, which direction effects flow, how strong correlations should be, and which method is appropriate.

Why layers disagree

A frequent surprise is that layers measured on the same samples correlate less than expected. That disagreement is usually biology, not a failed experiment:

  • Different timescales. Metabolites turn over in seconds, mRNAs in minutes to hours, proteins in hours to weeks. After a stimulus, mRNA peaks and falls before protein rises. Snapshot data at one time point capture different phases.
  • Post-transcriptional regulation. Translation efficiency, microRNAs, RNA-binding proteins and protein degradation (ubiquitin–proteasome, autophagy) all intervene between mRNA and protein.
  • Buffering and dosage compensation. Subunits of complexes are degraded when produced in excess, so an extra chromosome copy (aneuploidy) raises mRNA more than protein for complex members.
  • Enzyme kinetics. Flux depends on substrate concentration relative to Km, on allosteric control and on cofactors. A doubled enzyme often does not double flux.
  • Compartments and cell types. Bulk tissue mixes cells; a metabolite may come from a different cell type, or from microbes, than the enzyme you measured.
  • Technical noise and detection limits. Each platform measures a different subset with different dynamic range and missingness.
Evidence

Reviews of mRNA–protein relationships conclude that mRNA levels explain a substantial but incomplete part of protein variation across genes, and much less across samples for the same gene, with translation rates and protein turnover accounting for much of the rest (Liu, Beyer & Aebersold 2016, Cell; Buccitelli & Selbach 2020, Nature Reviews Genetics). In tumour cohorts (CPTAC), mRNA–protein correlation per gene across samples has a median around 0.4–0.5, lowest for stable housekeeping and complex proteins.

The QTL framework: genetics as the anchor

Genotype is fixed at conception and is not changed by disease, so associations from genotype to a molecular trait have a known causal direction. This makes molecular QTL mapping the backbone of most multi-omics interpretation:

  • Mapping: linear regression of normalised molecular phenotype on genotype dosage with covariates (genotype PCs, hidden factors from PEER or PCA). Tools: tensorQTL (GPU), QTLtools, FastQTL, MatrixEQTL; single-cell: pseudobulk per cell type or SAIGE-QTL, jaxQTL.
  • Resources: GTEx (49 tissues, eQTL and sQTL), eQTLGen (blood eQTLs, ~31,000 samples), the eQTL Catalogue (uniformly processed studies), OneK1K (single-cell blood eQTLs), UK Biobank PPP and deCODE (pQTLs), and the GoDMC (methylation QTLs).

From association to causation

Colocalisation

Do the GWAS and QTL signals share one causal variant, or are they two distinct variants in LD? coloc (Bayesian; posterior H4 for shared variant), coloc-SuSiE for multiple signals, eCAVIAR, HyPrColoc for many traits at once.

TWAS / PWAS

Predict gene expression (or protein) from genotype in a reference panel, then associate predicted expression with the trait in GWAS summary statistics (PrediXcan/S-PrediXcan, FUSION, UTMOST). Fast, but hits can reflect LD and co-regulation, not causal genes.

Mendelian randomisation

Use variants as instruments for an exposure (e.g. a cis-pQTL for a protein) to estimate its causal effect on an outcome. Assumes relevance, independence and no horizontal pleiotropy. Tools: TwoSampleMR, MendelianRandomization, MR-PRESSO, SMR with HEIDI test. Cis-pQTL MR is now used to prioritise drug targets.

Example: PCSK9 across the layers

Rare loss-of-function variants in PCSK9 lower LDL cholesterol and coronary disease risk (genome → phenome). PCSK9 protein binds the LDL receptor and targets it for degradation (proteome), so less PCSK9 means more receptors and lower plasma LDL (metabolome/lipidome). The genetics predicted that inhibiting PCSK9 would be safe and effective, and antibody (evolocumab, alirocumab) and siRNA (inclisiran) drugs followed. Drug programmes with human genetic support are roughly two to three times more likely to reach approval (Minikel et al. 2024, Nature).

Research gap
  • Many GWAS loci do not colocalise with any known eQTL ("missing regulation"); context-specific, single-cell and developmental QTLs may close the gap (Mostafavi et al. 2023, Nature Genetics).
  • Time-resolved multi-omics that captures lags between layers.
  • Causal models across more than two layers (variant → methylation → expression → protein → trait) with proper uncertainty.
  • Protein-to-metabolite links that account for flux, not just enzyme abundance.
Multi-omics · methods

Integrating multi-omics data

Integration means analysing two or more layers jointly so that the result is more than the sum of separate analyses. The right method depends on the question, on whether layers come from the same samples or cells, and on how much prior knowledge you want to impose.

When to combine: early, intermediate, late

Sample structure determines what is possible

Matched (vertical)

All layers measured on the same samples or cells. Allows true joint models (MOFA+, DIABLO, WNN, totalVI). The anchor is the sample.

Unmatched (diagonal)

Different samples or cells per layer, same biology. Needs feature-level links (gene activity from ATAC, prior graphs): GLUE, LIGER/UINMF, Seurat bridge integration. Riskier assumptions.

Mosaic

Some samples have some layers. Methods: MultiVI, StabMap, MIDAS, Cobolt. Common in real consortia data.

Horizontal

Same layer across batches or studies. This is batch integration (Harmony, scVI), not multi-omics, but usually comes first.

Core method families explained

Matrix factorisation and latent factor models

Each layer Xm (samples × features) is approximated as Z·WmT, where Z (samples × K factors) is shared and Wm holds layer-specific weights. MOFA/MOFA+ is a Bayesian group factor analysis with sparsity priors and per-layer likelihoods (Gaussian, Bernoulli, Poisson); it reports variance explained per factor per layer, so you see which factors are shared and which are private. JIVE separates joint from individual structure explicitly. MCIA and RGCCA maximise covariance between layer-specific projections. intNMF uses non-negative factorisation suited to clustering. iCluster/iClusterBayes fit a joint latent variable model for subtype discovery.

Similarity and network fusion

SNF builds a patient-similarity network per layer and iteratively fuses them through message passing until they converge; spectral clustering of the fused network gives subtypes. Robust to different scales and noise, easy to apply, but feature-level interpretation is indirect. NEMO extends this to partial data.

Supervised multi-block methods

DIABLO (mixOmics) finds sparse components that are correlated across layers and discriminate a categorical outcome, controlled by a design matrix of how strongly layers should correlate. Good for biomarker panels with cross-validation. Multi-block sPLS and penalised regression with layer-specific penalties (priority-Lasso, IPF-Lasso) are alternatives.

Deep generative models

Variational autoencoders with modality-specific encoders and decoders learn a joint latent space while modelling count noise properly: totalVI (RNA + surface proteins), MultiVI (RNA + ATAC, with mosaic support), PeakVI, GLUE (uses a prior regulatory graph to link unpaired modalities), scMM, MIDAS. Bulk-level examples include multi-omics autoencoders for survival (e.g. in TCGA). Graph neural networks operate on biological networks with omics features as node attributes. These models are flexible but need more data and careful evaluation against simple baselines.

Knowledge-driven integration

Instead of letting the data discover structure, map all layers onto prior networks: pathways (Reactome, KEGG), signalling (OmniPath), metabolic models (Human-GEM), regulatory networks (CollecTRI). COSMOS links phosphoproteomics, transcriptomics and metabolomics through a causal prior knowledge network using integer linear programming. Omics Integrator solves a prize-collecting Steiner forest to connect measured hits through the interactome. ActivePathways merges p-values across layers per pathway. These are more interpretable but limited by the completeness and bias of the prior knowledge.

A practical recipe

  1. Write down the question and the relationship you expect (previous chapter). Pick the method family from the task table.
  2. Process each layer well on its own first: QC, normalisation, batch correction, and single-layer results. If a layer is uninformative alone, integration rarely rescues it.
  3. Harmonise samples and identifiers: one sample sheet with IDs per layer; check sample identity across layers (genotype concordance from RNA and DNA, sex markers) to detect swaps.
  4. Transform to comparable scales: variance-stabilised counts, log intensities, M-values, CLR for compositions. Scale features or weight layers so the largest layer does not dominate.
  5. Select features (highly variable per layer) to keep layers balanced and reduce noise.
  6. Handle missing data explicitly: methods that tolerate missing views (MOFA+, MultiVI, late fusion) are better than imputing whole layers.
  7. Fit, then inspect: variance explained per layer, factor–covariate associations (batch must not drive factors), stability across random seeds and subsamples.
  8. Interpret with feature weights, enrichment per factor, and links back to known biology.
  9. Validate on an independent cohort or with an orthogonal experiment. For prediction, nest feature selection inside cross-validation to avoid leakage.

Tools

Benchmarks

The honest summary of the benchmarks

No integration method wins everywhere. Adding layers does not automatically improve subtyping or prediction; a well-processed single layer (often transcriptomics) can match multi-omics models. Gains are largest when layers carry complementary information and sample sizes are adequate. Simple baselines (concatenated PCA, late fusion of separately trained models) should always be reported.

Research gap
  • Benchmarks with real ground truth for multi-omics (spike-in designs, simulations calibrated to real data, perturbation experiments).
  • Integration that respects causal direction and time, rather than symmetric correlation.
  • Uncertainty quantification for latent factors and integrated predictions.
  • Scalable, privacy-preserving (federated) integration across cohorts that cannot share data.
  • Interpretable deep integration: tying latent dimensions back to pathways and mechanisms.
  • Mosaic integration with many missing layers, typical of clinical cohorts.
Practice

Study design, workflows and reproducibility

Most failed omics projects fail in design or data management, not in algorithms. This chapter covers the practical layer: design, batch effects, file formats, public data, workflow managers and reporting.

Design before data

  • Power: estimate from pilot or public data (RNA-seq: RNASeqPower, PROPER, ssizeRNA; scRNA-seq: scPower, hierarchicell; GWAS: genpwr). Biological replicates beat sequencing depth.
  • Randomise and block: spread conditions across extraction days, library batches, flow cells and MS runs. Record everything in sample metadata.
  • Controls: spike-ins (ERCC, SIRVs, sequins), pooled QC samples, reference materials (GIAB cell lines, Quartet multi-omics reference materials, NIST plasma), negative controls (blanks).
  • Matched multi-omics: split the same aliquot across assays where possible; time from collection to freezing matters most for RNA and metabolites.

Batch effects

If batch is not confounded with the variable of interest, include it as a covariate in the model (preferred) or correct it: ComBat (empirical Bayes on continuous data), ComBat-seq (counts), limma removeBatchEffect (for visualisation only), RUVSeq/RUVg (using control genes), SVA (surrogate variables for unknown batches), and for single-cell, Harmony or scVI. Check before and after with PCA coloured by batch and by biology, kBET or LISI scores, and silhouette widths.

Pitfall

Correcting batch and then testing for group differences with the corrected data can exaggerate significance, especially when the design is unbalanced (Nygaard et al. 2016, Biostatistics). Model batch in the test instead of pre-correcting when possible.

Reproducible computation

  • Workflow managers: Nextflow with the community-curated nf-core pipelines (rnaseq, sarek, atacseq, methylseq, scrnaseq, mag, ampliseq and more), Snakemake, WDL/Cromwell (Terra, GATK), CWL.
  • Environments: conda/mamba (bioconda), Docker, Apptainer/Singularity for HPC; renv (R) and uv/poetry (Python) for analysis code.
  • Version control: git for code; record reference genome build, annotation release, tool versions and parameters. DVC or data registries for large data.
  • Notebooks and reports: Quarto, R Markdown, Jupyter. Keep exploratory notebooks separate from the final, scripted analysis.
  • FAIR data: findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable. Use community metadata standards (MINSEQE, MIAPE, MIxS, Metabolomics Standards Initiative).
  • Compute: WGS alignment and calling ≈ hours per genome on a server (minutes on GPU with Parabricks or DRAGEN); scRNA-seq with ~106 cells needs tens of GB of RAM or out-of-core tools; deep models need GPUs.
