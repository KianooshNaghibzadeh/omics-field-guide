FoundationsChapter 1

Genome, genes & chromatin

Before any algorithm makes sense you need a clear picture of the molecules being measured: what DNA is, how a gene is laid out, and how two metres of DNA fit inside a nucleus while staying readable.

DNA: the molecule

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a polymer of four nucleotides. Each nucleotide has a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate and one of four bases: the purines adenine (A) and guanine (G), and the pyrimidines cytosine (C) and thymine (T). Two strands wind into a right-handed double helix and pair by hydrogen bonds: A with T (two bonds) and G with C (three bonds). Because G–C pairs are stronger, the GC content of a region affects its melting temperature, how well it is captured and amplified in library preparation, and therefore sequencing coverage. GC bias is a recurring source of technical artefacts in nearly every sequencing-based omics assay.

The strands are antiparallel: one runs 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′. Polymerases only synthesise in the 5′→3′ direction, so by convention sequences are written 5′→3′. A gene can sit on either strand, which is why annotation files (GTF/GFF) carry a + or − strand field and why "strand-specific" RNA-seq libraries matter: they tell you which strand an RNA was transcribed from, so overlapping antisense genes can be separated.

Key idea

Base complementarity is the physical principle behind most of the field: hybridisation arrays, PCR, probe capture, bisulfite conversion readouts, CRISPR guide targeting and sequencing-by-synthesis all depend on A·T and G·C pairing.

Anatomy of a protein-coding gene

Reading 5′→3′ along the coding strand, a typical eukaryotic gene contains:

  • Promoter: the region around the transcription start site (TSS) where RNA polymerase II and general transcription factors assemble. Core promoter elements include the TATA box (~25–30 bp upstream, in a minority of genes), the initiator and downstream promoter element. About 60–70% of human promoters sit in CpG islands, regions of high CpG density that are usually unmethylated when the gene is active.
  • Enhancers and silencers: distal regulatory elements, sometimes hundreds of kilobases away, bound by sequence-specific transcription factors. They contact promoters through 3D chromatin looping. Most disease-associated GWAS variants fall in enhancers rather than in coding sequence.
  • 5′ untranslated region (5′UTR): transcribed but not translated; contains elements controlling ribosome recruitment, including upstream open reading frames (uORFs).
  • Exons and introns: exons are retained in mature mRNA; introns are removed by splicing. Human genes average ~9–10 exons; introns are on average much longer than exons.
  • Coding sequence (CDS): from the start codon (AUG) to a stop codon (UAA, UAG, UGA), read in triplets.
  • 3′UTR and polyadenylation signal: the AAUAAA hexamer directs cleavage and poly(A) tail addition. 3′UTRs carry microRNA binding sites and RNA-binding protein motifs that control stability, localisation and translation. Alternative polyadenylation produces isoforms with longer or shorter 3′UTRs.

Chromatin: packaging that controls access

DNA wraps ~147 bp at a time around a nucleosome, an octamer of histones H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. Nucleosomes are separated by linker DNA (bound by H1), giving the "beads on a string" fibre with a ~200 bp periodicity. This periodicity is visible directly in ATAC-seq fragment-length distributions and in cell-free DNA fragment sizes (~167 bp peak), which is why both are used to infer nucleosome positioning.

Chromatin exists in two broad states:

Euchromatin

Open, gene-rich, transcriptionally active. Marked by H3K4me3 at active promoters, H3K27ac at active enhancers and promoters, H3K36me3 across transcribed gene bodies. Accessible to transcription factors and to Tn5 transposase (ATAC-seq) and DNase I.

Heterochromatin

Compact and repressed. Constitutive (H3K9me3; repeats, centromeres) or facultative (H3K27me3 deposited by Polycomb; developmentally silenced genes that can be reactivated). Usually high CpG methylation outside islands.

At larger scales the genome folds into loops (often anchored by CTCF and cohesin), topologically associating domains (TADs) of roughly 100 kb–1 Mb that preferentially self-interact, and A/B compartments that separate active from inactive chromatin. These are measured with chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C, Micro-C) and covered in the epigenomics chapter. Disrupting a TAD boundary can let an enhancer activate the wrong gene; this mechanism explains some limb malformations and cancers.

Cells, tissues and why context matters

Almost every cell in a person carries the same genome, yet a neuron and a hepatocyte use very different sets of genes. Cell identity is established by transcription factors and maintained by the epigenome. For analysis this has three consequences:

  1. Bulk measurements average over cell types. A change in a bulk tissue profile can mean that cells changed state, or only that the cell-type proportions changed. Deconvolution and single-cell methods exist to separate these.
  2. Regulatory effects are context-specific. A variant may affect expression only in one cell type, developmental stage or stimulus condition, which is a central difficulty when interpreting GWAS hits.
  3. Somatic variation exists. Cells acquire mutations as they divide. Cancer is the extreme case, but normal tissues are also mosaics of clones, which matters for low-frequency variant detection.
Pitfall

Gene identifiers are a constant source of silent data loss. HGNC symbols change (for example, SEPT7 → SEPTIN7, after spreadsheets kept converting it to a date), Ensembl IDs carry version suffixes (ENSG00000141510.18), and one gene maps to many transcripts and proteins. Map on stable IDs, strip versions consistently and keep the mapping table you used.

FoundationsChapter 2

The central dogma, step by step

Francis Crick's 1958 statement is about the direction of sequence information: it can pass from nucleic acid to nucleic acid and from nucleic acid to protein, but never from protein back to nucleic acid. Each transfer step is a control point, an assay, and a place where mutations act.

Replication

Before a cell divides it copies its genome. DNA polymerases δ and ε synthesise new strands with an intrinsic error rate of roughly 10−4–10−5, which proofreading (3′→5′ exonuclease) and mismatch repair (MMR) lower to about 10−9–10−10 per base per division. When proofreading (POLE, POLD1 mutations) or MMR (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) fails, tumours become hypermutated or microsatellite-unstable. These tumours carry characteristic mutational signatures and respond well to immune checkpoint inhibitors because their many mutations create neoantigens. That chain from a repair defect to a sequencing signature to a treatment decision is one of the clearest examples of omics in the clinic.

Transcription

RNA polymerase II reads the template strand and produces RNA that matches the coding strand (with U in place of T). Transcription has distinct, separately regulated phases:

  1. Initiation: transcription factors bind enhancers and promoters; the Mediator complex bridges them to the pre-initiation complex (TFIID with TATA-binding protein, TFIIB, TFIIE, TFIIF, TFIIH). TFIIH phosphorylates serine 5 of the Pol II C-terminal domain (CTD).
  2. Promoter-proximal pausing: in metazoans Pol II often pauses 20–60 nt downstream of the TSS. Release by P-TEFb (CDK9) is a major regulatory step, which is why nascent-transcription assays (PRO-seq, GRO-seq, TT-seq) can reveal regulation that steady-state RNA-seq misses.
  3. Elongation: CTD serine 2 phosphorylation recruits splicing and 3′-end factors. Splicing is largely co-transcriptional, so elongation speed influences which exons are included.
  4. Termination: cleavage at the poly(A) site, poly(A) addition, and release of Pol II.

Three other polymerases have dedicated jobs: Pol I makes ribosomal RNA, Pol III makes tRNAs, 5S rRNA and some small RNAs, and mitochondrial RNA polymerase transcribes the mitochondrial genome. Ribosomal RNA is 80% or more of total RNA, which is why RNA-seq libraries either select poly(A)+ RNA or deplete rRNA. The choice changes what you can see: poly(A) selection loses histone mRNAs, many lncRNAs and most intronic reads, while rRNA depletion keeps them.

RNA processing

5′ capping

A 7-methylguanosine cap is added co-transcriptionally. It protects the RNA and recruits eIF4E for translation. Cap-based protocols (CAGE) map transcription start sites precisely.

Splicing

Introns are removed and exons joined by the spliceosome. More than 90–95% of multi-exon human genes are alternatively spliced. See the splicing chapter.

3′ end processing

Cleavage and polyadenylation. About 70% of human genes have more than one poly(A) site (alternative polyadenylation, APA), changing 3′UTR length and therefore microRNA regulation.

Editing and modification

A-to-I editing by ADAR enzymes (read as A→G mismatches in RNA-seq), C-to-U editing by APOBEC1, and over 170 chemical modifications such as m6A, pseudouridine and m5C. This is the epitranscriptome.

Export and decay

Mature mRNAs are exported through nuclear pores. Decay pathways (deadenylation, decapping, exonucleases, nonsense-mediated decay) set mRNA half-lives from minutes to days.

Non-coding RNA biogenesis

microRNAs are processed by Drosha and Dicer into ~22-nt guides loaded into Argonaute; circular RNAs arise from back-splicing; lncRNAs are capped and often polyadenylated like mRNAs.

Translation and the genetic code

Ribosomes scan from the 5′ cap to the first suitable AUG (in a favourable Kozak context, gccAccAUGG) and read the mRNA three nucleotides at a time. With 43 = 64 codons for 20 amino acids plus stop, the code is degenerate: most amino acids have several codons that usually differ at the third ("wobble") position. That is why many third-position changes are synonymous, and why the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions (dN/dS) is used to detect selection and cancer driver genes.

Translation has three phases: initiation (the most regulated, through eIF2 and eIF4F; the integrated stress response shuts it down by phosphorylating eIF2α), elongation (codon choice and tRNA availability affect speed and co-translational folding), and termination at a stop codon by eRF1/eRF3. Translation efficiency is measured by ribosome profiling (Ribo-seq), which sequences the ~30-nt mRNA fragments protected by ribosomes.

After translation: the protein is not finished

Polypeptides fold (often with chaperones), may be cleaved (signal peptides, pro-hormones), and receive post-translational modifications (PTMs): phosphorylation (S, T, Y; signalling), acetylation (K; chromatin and metabolism), ubiquitination (K; degradation and signalling), glycosylation (N, S, T; secreted and membrane proteins), methylation, SUMOylation, lipidation and hundreds more. One gene can therefore give rise to many proteoforms, through alternative splicing, coding variants and PTM combinations. Protein half-lives range from minutes to months. Proteomics measures a cell state that is related to, but not determined by, the transcriptome.

Why the dogma is a network

The arrows look linear, but every step is controlled by products of later steps: transcription factors, splicing factors, RNA-binding proteins and chromatin modifiers are all proteins, and metabolites feed back too. Acetyl-CoA supplies histone acetylation, S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) supplies DNA and histone methylation, and α-ketoglutarate is a co-factor for TET demethylases. Mutations in the metabolic enzymes IDH1/IDH2 produce the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate, which inhibits TET and histone demethylases and causes a hypermethylation phenotype in glioma and leukaemia. In one example, a genomic variant changes a metabolite, the metabolite rewires the epigenome, and the epigenome alters the transcriptome. Multi-omics integration exists to trace chains like this.

Key idea

The correlation between mRNA and protein levels across genes in a steady-state cell is moderate (often around 0.4–0.6 in mammalian datasets), and across samples for the same gene it is often weaker. Translation rates, protein degradation and time delays explain much of the gap. Do not use a transcriptome as a stand-in for the proteome without checking. See How the layers talk.

Exceptions and extensions worth knowing

  • Reverse transcription: retroviruses (HIV), LINE-1 retrotransposons and telomerase write RNA back into DNA. Processed pseudogenes in the genome are fossils of this.
  • RNA-dependent RNA synthesis: RNA viruses (SARS-CoV-2, influenza) replicate RNA directly.
  • Prions: misfolded proteins template the misfolding of others. This passes conformation, not sequence, so it does not violate the dogma.
  • Non-coding RNAs as end products: rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, miRNA and lncRNA function without being translated.
  • Small ORFs and micropeptides: some "non-coding" RNAs encode short functional peptides that Ribo-seq and proteomics are still uncovering.
  • Epigenetic inheritance: methylation and chromatin states can be transmitted through cell divisions without changes in DNA sequence.

Splicing: one gene, many messages

Splicing removes introns from pre-mRNA and joins exons. Alternative splicing lets roughly 20,000 genes produce well over 100,000 transcript isoforms. It is also one of the most underestimated routes by which mutations cause disease.

Additional signals help: exonic and intronic splicing enhancers and silencers (ESE, ESS, ISE, ISS) recruit activating SR proteins (SRSF1–12) or repressing hnRNPs. Tissue-specific regulators (RBFOX, NOVA, PTBP1/2, MBNL, CELF, ESRP) create neural, muscle or epithelial splicing programmes. Chromatin and Pol II elongation speed matter too: slow elongation gives weak exons more time to be recognised.

The spliceosome's two chemical steps

  1. Branching: the 2′-OH of the branch point adenosine attacks the 5′ splice site, freeing exon 1 and forming a lariat intron.
  2. Exon ligation: the free 3′-OH of exon 1 attacks the 3′ splice site, joining the exons and releasing the lariat, which is debranched and degraded.

The spliceosome assembles stepwise from five small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (U1, U2, U4, U5, U6) and more than 150 proteins. Exon junction complexes (EJCs) are deposited ~20–24 nt upstream of each junction, which links splicing to mRNA export, translation and nonsense-mediated decay.

Consequences: nonsense-mediated decay and protein diversity

When an isoform contains a premature termination codon (PTC) more than ~50–55 nt upstream of the last exon–exon junction, a downstream EJC remains on the mRNA after the first round of translation and triggers nonsense-mediated decay (NMD). Cells use this deliberately: "poison exons" containing PTCs are included to downregulate genes such as splicing factors themselves (alternative splicing coupled to NMD, AS-NMD). In analysis, NMD means a frameshifting or exon-skipping variant often reduces RNA abundance rather than producing a truncated protein. This is why allele-specific expression in RNA-seq is used to confirm loss-of-function variants.

Isoforms that do get translated can differ in domains, localisation signals or interaction surfaces, sometimes with opposite functions (Bcl-xL vs Bcl-xS). How many alternative isoforms produce stable, functional proteins remains an open question; mass spectrometry detects far fewer protein isoforms than RNA-seq predicts, partly because of sensitivity and partly because many isoforms may be noise.

Why splicing mutations matter

Estimates vary by method, but a substantial fraction of disease-causing variants act through splicing, from roughly 10% when only canonical ±1/±2 sites are counted to well over 20% when deep intronic and exonic splicing variants are included. Mechanisms:

  • Canonical site loss (GT→AT at +1): usually exon skipping or intron retention, often leading to NMD.
  • Cryptic site activation: a variant creates a new donor or acceptor deep in an intron, adding a pseudo-exon (common in CFTR, DMD, ATM).
  • Exonic variants that disrupt ESEs/ESSs: a "synonymous" change can alter splicing. Synonymous does not mean silent.
  • Branch point and polypyrimidine tract variants, which are often missed by tools that only examine ±8 nt from exon boundaries.
  • Trans-acting mutations in spliceosome genes (SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, ZRSR2) in myelodysplastic syndromes and other cancers alter thousands of events at once.
  • Repeat expansions that sequester splicing regulators, as CUG repeats in DMPK trap MBNL in myotonic dystrophy type 1.
Example: splicing as a drug target

In spinal muscular atrophy, SMN1 is lost and the near-identical SMN2 mostly skips exon 7 because of a single C→T change that weakens an enhancer. The antisense oligonucleotide nusinersen blocks an intronic silencer (ISS-N1) and the small molecule risdiplam stabilises U1 binding at the exon 7 donor. Both restore exon 7 inclusion. These are among the clearest demonstrations that understanding a splicing mechanism at nucleotide resolution can lead to a therapy.

Computational analysis of splicing

From RNA-seq

  • Splice-aware alignment (STAR, HISAT2) finds reads that span junctions. Use two-pass mode for novel junctions. Long reads (minimap2 -ax splice) capture whole isoforms.
  • Event-level quantification: rMATS, SUPPA2, MAJIQ, Whippet and VAST-TOOLS compute PSI and test for differential splicing.
  • Annotation-free intron clustering: LeafCutter groups introns that share splice sites and tests intron-usage ratios; widely used for sQTL mapping in GTEx.
  • Exon-bin or isoform-level: DEXSeq (differential exon usage), DRIMSeq, satuRn and IsoformSwitchAnalyzeR (functional consequences of isoform switches).
  • Single-cell splicing: full-length protocols (Smart-seq2/3) or long-read single cell; 3′-tag 10x data are largely blind to internal splicing.

From DNA sequence (variant effect prediction)

  • SpliceAI (deep residual CNN over 10 kb context) predicts donor/acceptor gain and loss. Delta score ≥ 0.2 is a common screening threshold and ≥ 0.5 is high-confidence.
  • Pangolin (tissue-aware), MMSplice (modular), SQUIRLS, SPiP, MaxEntScan (classic position-weight/maximum-entropy scorer).
  • Clinical laboratories increasingly validate predicted splice variants with RNA-seq from blood or fibroblasts, or with minigene assays, before classification.
Benchmark

Systematic comparisons of differential splicing tools report low overlap between tools and strong dependence on read depth, replicate number and event type (Mehmood et al. 2020, Briefings in Bioinformatics). For splice-variant prediction, SpliceAI and related deep models are consistently among the top performers on clinical and MPRA-based sets, while older motif scorers lose accuracy outside canonical positions. Results depend on how far into the intron variants lie.

Research gap
  • Linking isoforms to function: which of the thousands of detected isoforms are translated and matter?
  • Cell-type-resolved splicing from droplet single-cell data, where coverage of internal exons is poor.
  • Predicting the quantitative effect of a variant (ΔPSI) in a specific tissue, not just whether a site is lost or gained.
  • Calibrated splice predictions for clinical use, especially deep intronic and branch-point variants.

Mutations and genetic variation

A mutation matters because of where in the information flow it acts. The same single-base change can do nothing, break a protein, shift a splice site, disable an enhancer or change a drug's metabolism. This chapter classifies variants and traces their consequences through every layer.

Terms that are often confused

Mutation
A change in DNA sequence relative to a parent cell or organism; an event.
Variant
A position where an individual differs from a reference sequence. Preferred neutral term in clinical genetics.
Polymorphism
A variant common in a population (conventionally minor allele frequency ≥ 1%).
Allele
One version of a sequence at a locus. Humans are diploid: two alleles per autosomal locus.
Genotype / haplotype
The pair of alleles at a locus / the set of alleles on one chromosome copy. Phasing assigns alleles to haplotypes.
Germline / somatic
Inherited and present in every cell / acquired after conception and present only in a cell lineage (tumours, clonal haematopoiesis, mosaicism).
Zygosity
Heterozygous (one copy), homozygous (two), hemizygous (one copy present, e.g. X in males), compound heterozygous (two different variants on different copies).

Non-coding consequences

  • Promoter: TERT promoter mutations (C228T, C250T) create ETS-factor binding sites and reactivate telomerase in melanoma, glioblastoma and bladder cancer.
  • Enhancer: a variant in the ZRS enhancer, ~1 Mb from SHH, causes preaxial polydactyly. The FTO obesity locus acts through enhancers that regulate IRX3/IRX5, not FTO itself.
  • UTRs: variants that create upstream start codons, or that destroy microRNA sites or polyadenylation signals.
  • Structural and 3D: deleting a TAD boundary lets enhancers reach the wrong gene (enhancer hijacking).
  • Non-coding RNA genes: variants in RNU4ATAC (minor spliceosome snRNA) cause developmental disorders; a recurrent RNU4-2 variant is a recently described common cause of neurodevelopmental disorder.

Worked example: lactase persistence

The variant rs4988235 (−13910 C>T) sits in an intron of MCM6, ~14 kb upstream of LCT. The T allele creates an OCT1 binding site in an enhancer (epigenome), keeps LCT transcription active into adulthood (transcriptome), maintains lactase enzyme in the intestinal brush border (proteome), allows lactose digestion into glucose and galactose (metabolome), and produces lactose tolerance (phenome). It was strongly selected in populations with a history of dairying. One variant, six layers.

Somatic mutations and cancer

A tumour is a clonal expansion carrying driver mutations, which confer a growth advantage, and many more passenger mutations, which are neutral hitchhikers. Analyses specific to somatic data include:

  • Tumour–normal paired calling to remove germline variants (Mutect2, Strelka2), with panels of normals to remove recurrent artefacts.
  • Variant allele fraction (VAF) adjusted for purity and copy number to infer clonality and reconstruct phylogenies (PyClone-VI, SciClone, PhyloWGS).
  • Driver discovery through recurrence beyond the background mutation rate (MutSigCV, dNdScv, OncodriveFML), which must correct for replication timing, expression and local sequence context.
  • Mutational signatures: the 96-channel SBS spectrum (6 substitution types × 16 trinucleotide contexts) is decomposed into signatures with known causes. Examples: SBS4 (tobacco smoking, C>A), SBS7 (UV light, C>T at dipyrimidines), SBS2/13 (APOBEC), SBS3 (homologous-recombination deficiency, relevant for PARP inhibitors), SBS1 (clock-like deamination of 5-methylcytosine). Tools: SigProfiler, deconstructSigs, MutationalPatterns, SigLASSO.
  • Tumour mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) as biomarkers for immunotherapy; HLA typing and neoantigen prediction (NetMHCpan, pVACtools).

Interpreting a germline variant

The ACMG/AMP 2015 guidelines combine evidence types into five classes: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, benign. Evidence types include population frequency (gnomAD; a variant too common for the disease prevalence is benign), computational predictions, functional data, segregation in families, de novo status, and the gene–disease relationship (ClinGen). ClinGen's 2022 calibration of computational predictors (Pejaver et al.) specifies score thresholds for tools such as REVEL and BayesDel so they can count as supporting, moderate or strong evidence.

Missense predictors

SIFT and PolyPhen-2 (classic), CADD (genome-wide integrative score), REVEL and BayesDel (ensembles), PrimateAI-3D, EVE and ESM-1v (evolutionary and protein language models, trained without clinical labels), AlphaMissense (AlphaFold-derived, 2023).

Constraint metrics

pLI and LOEUF (gnomAD) measure how strongly a gene tolerates loss-of-function variants. Missense constraint (MPC) and regional constraint highlight intolerant domains. GERP, phyloP and phastCons measure evolutionary conservation per base.

Experimental maps

Deep mutational scanning (DMS) and saturation genome editing (SGE; e.g. BRCA1, Findlay et al. 2018) measure the effect of nearly every possible SNV in a region. MaveDB collects these maps, which are increasingly used as functional evidence.

Pitfall

Predictors trained on ClinVar and evaluated on ClinVar look better than they are, because the same variants or genes leak between training and test sets and because clinical classifications already used older predictors (Grimm et al. 2015, Human Mutation). Prefer evaluations on deep mutational scanning data or on newly classified variants.

Pharmacogenomics

Germline variants in drug-metabolising enzymes and transporters change drug exposure. Examples with clinical guidelines (CPIC, DPWG): CYP2D6 (codeine, tamoxifen; star-allele haplotypes including gene deletions, duplications and hybrids), CYP2C19 (clopidogrel), TPMT/NUDT15 (thiopurines), DPYD (fluoropyrimidines), HLA-B*57:01 (abacavir hypersensitivity), SLCO1B1 (statin myopathy). Calling star alleles requires specialised tools (Aldy, Stargazer, PharmCAT, Cyrius) because of paralogs and structural variants.

Population genetics you will need

  • Allele frequency and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: strong deviations in controls often indicate genotyping error.
  • Linkage disequilibrium (LD): nearby variants are inherited together, so a GWAS signal marks a region, not a causal variant. Fine-mapping (SuSiE, FINEMAP) assigns posterior probabilities to variants within the region.
  • Population structure and ancestry: control with principal components or mixed models, otherwise allele-frequency differences between groups create false associations. About 85% or more of GWAS participants have been of European ancestry, which reduces the portability of polygenic scores to other groups.
  • Effect size versus frequency: rare variants can have large effects (Mendelian disease); common variants usually have small effects (complex traits). Both contribute to heritability.
Research gap
  • Most VUSs are missense or non-coding. Accurate, calibrated prediction for non-coding variants is largely unsolved.
  • Cell-type- and context-specific variant effects, including variants that act only under stimulation or at particular developmental stages.
  • Equitable references: allele frequencies, LD panels and predictor training data remain skewed toward European ancestry.
  • Somatic mosaicism in normal tissues: detecting variants at VAF below 1% without error-corrected sequencing.

Three properties that shape every analysis

p ≫ n

Far more features (p) than samples (n). Classical regression breaks down; you need regularisation, dimensionality reduction, empirical Bayes shrinkage and false discovery rate (FDR) control (Benjamini–Hochberg, Storey's q-value).

Measurements are relative

Sequencing returns a fixed number of reads per library, so counts are compositional: one feature rising forces others down. Normalisation (size factors, TMM, CLR) tries to recover comparable scales. Spike-ins can restore absolute scale.

Technical variation is large

Batch, operator, reagent lot, run date and sample handling can explain more variance than biology. Randomise, block and record; correct afterwards only when the design allows it.

The generic analysis arc

Every layer follows a similar arc, even though the tools differ:

  1. Design: question, replicates, power, randomisation, controls, metadata standards.
  2. Primary processing: instrument signal to reads or spectra; QC (FastQC, MultiQC; PTXQC for proteomics).
  3. Secondary processing: reads to features (alignment, quantification, variant/peak calling, identification).
  4. Feature-level QC and normalisation: filter low-quality samples and features, correct library size or loading, examine batch with PCA.
  5. Statistical modelling: differential abundance, association, clustering, prediction.
  6. Interpretation: annotation, enrichment, networks, integration with other layers and with prior knowledge.
  7. Validation and sharing: independent cohort, orthogonal assay, data deposition (GEO, PRIDE, MetaboLights, EGA).

Genomics

The germline short-read pipeline

  1. QC and trimming (FastQC, fastp): adapters, quality, duplication.
  2. Alignment to GRCh38 (BWA-MEM2; minimap2 for long reads). Uses the Burrows–Wheeler transform/FM-index to find exact seed matches quickly, then Smith–Waterman local alignment. Output: SAM/BAM/CRAM with mapping quality (MAPQ = −10 log10 P(mapped position wrong)).
  3. Duplicate marking (Picard, samblaster, GATK MarkDuplicatesSpark): PCR and optical duplicates inflate confidence.
  4. Base quality score recalibration (BQSR): models systematic quality errors by machine cycle and context. Less important with modern instruments and with DeepVariant.
  5. Variant calling: GATK HaplotypeCaller re-assembles local haplotypes with a de Bruijn graph and computes genotype likelihoods with a pair-HMM; DeepVariant encodes read pileups as images and classifies genotypes with a CNN; Strelka2, FreeBayes, Octopus and DRAGEN are alternatives. Cohort calling uses gVCFs and joint genotyping (GATK GenotypeGVCFs, GLnexus).
  6. Filtering: VQSR (Gaussian mixture on annotations) for large cohorts, hard filters for small ones, or learned filters.
  7. Annotation: VEP, ANNOVAR, SnpEff add gene, transcript and consequence, population frequency (gnomAD), predictions (CADD, REVEL, SpliceAI, AlphaMissense) and clinical databases (ClinVar).
  8. Prioritisation: Exomiser and LIRICAL combine variant scores with patient phenotypes (HPO terms); inheritance-mode filters for trios.
Key idea

Variant calling is a statistical inference, not a lookup. A genotype call is the most likely genotype given read evidence, base and mapping qualities and a prior. Report and filter on genotype quality (GQ), depth (DP) and allele balance, and use benchmark regions to know where calls can be trusted.

Structural variants and copy number

Short-read SV callers combine three signals: discordant read pairs (unexpected insert size or orientation), split reads (one read spanning a breakpoint), and read depth. Assembly-based callers reconstruct breakpoint sequence. Manta, DELLY, LUMPY/smoove and GRIDSS2 are widely used, often merged with SURVIVOR or Jasmine. Long-read callers (Sniffles2, cuteSV, pbsv, SVIM) and assembly-based comparison (PAV, SVIM-asm, dipcall) recover far more SVs, especially insertions in repeats. For copy number: CNVkit and GATK gCNV (exomes and panels), CNVnator and Canvas (WGS), ASCAT, FACETS and PURPLE (tumour allele-specific copy number with purity and ploidy), and GISTIC2 (recurrent CNAs across tumours).

Somatic variant calling

Tumour samples are mixtures of tumour and normal cells, often with subclones, so somatic variants may appear at 5% VAF or lower. Use a matched normal, a panel of normals, and germline resource filtering (Mutect2 with FilterMutectCalls, Strelka2, VarDict, LoFreq). FFPE samples cause C>T deamination artefacts, and oxidation causes G>T artefacts; orientation-bias filters address both. Ultra-sensitive applications (minimal residual disease, liquid biopsy) need unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and duplex sequencing to suppress errors below 10−6.

De novo assembly and pangenomes

When the reference is incomplete or absent, reads are assembled. Overlap-layout-consensus and string graphs (hifiasm, HiCanu, Flye, Shasta) handle long reads; de Bruijn graphs (SPAdes, MEGAHIT) handle short reads. Hybrid approaches (Verkko) combine HiFi with ultra-long ONT for telomere-to-telomere assemblies; Hi-C or parental data (trio binning) phase haplotypes. Assess with N50, BUSCO completeness, Merqury k-mer QV and Inspector. The Human Pangenome Reference Consortium builds graph references (minigraph-Cactus, PGGB) that reduce reference bias; vg giraffe aligns reads to graphs.

Population and statistical genetics

  • GWAS: test each variant for association with a trait while controlling population structure. PLINK 2 for QC and simple models; mixed models handle relatedness and structure at biobank scale (REGENIE, SAIGE, BOLT-LMM, fastGWA). Genome-wide significance is conventionally p < 5×10−8.
  • Imputation: infer untyped variants from haplotype reference panels (TOPMed, 1000 Genomes, HRC) with Beagle 5, Minimac4 or IMPUTE5; pre-phasing with SHAPEIT5 or Eagle2.
  • Heritability and genetic correlation: LD score regression (LDSC), GCTA-GREML, S-LDSC for enrichment in annotations (cell-type-specific enhancers).
  • Fine-mapping: SuSiE, FINEMAP, PolyFun (functional priors) output credible sets of likely causal variants.
  • Polygenic scores: PRS-CS, LDpred2, SBayesRC, PRS-CSx (multi-ancestry). Evaluate with incremental R² or AUC in held-out cohorts of each ancestry.
  • Rare-variant burden tests: SKAT-O, ACAT, gene-based burden in exome biobanks.

Epigenomics

Heritable or stable changes in gene activity that do not change DNA sequence: methylation of DNA, chemical marks on histones, which DNA is accessible, where transcription factors sit, and how chromosomes fold in 3D. Epigenomics explains how one genome makes hundreds of cell types.

Research questions

  • Which regulatory elements are active in this cell type, and which genes do they control?
  • How do disease-associated variants change TF binding, accessibility or methylation?
  • Does methylation mark disease state, ageing (epigenetic clocks), exposure (smoking) or cell composition?
  • Can methylation patterns in cell-free DNA detect cancer and identify its tissue of origin?
  • How does 3D genome folding (loops, TADs) control enhancer–promoter communication?

DNA methylation

In mammals methylation is mostly 5-methylcytosine (5mC) at CpG dinucleotides. About 70–80% of CpGs are methylated, except in CpG islands at active promoters. DNMT3A/B establish new methylation, DNMT1 maintains it through replication, and TET enzymes oxidise 5mC to 5hmC, 5fC and 5caC on the way to demethylation. Promoter methylation is associated with silencing; gene-body methylation correlates with active transcription.

Epigenetic clocks

Penalised regression on methylation predicts age with striking accuracy: Horvath's multi-tissue clock (353 CpGs, 2013) and Hannum's blood clock. Later clocks predict health outcomes rather than chronological age: PhenoAge, GrimAge and the pace-of-ageing measure DunedinPACE. Their biological meaning, and whether interventions that change them change health, are open questions.

Chromatin accessibility

ATAC-seq uses hyperactive Tn5 transposase to insert sequencing adapters into open chromatin. It needs ~50,000 cells (or single nuclei in scATAC). Fragment lengths show nucleosome-free (<100 bp), mono-nucleosome (~200 bp) and di-nucleosome peaks; the TSS enrichment score and fragment-size periodicity are the main QC metrics. DNase-seq and MNase-seq are older alternatives.

Pipeline: trim (Nextera adapters), align (Bowtie2 or BWA), remove mitochondrial reads and duplicates, shift reads +4/−5 bp for Tn5 offset, call peaks (MACS2/3, Genrich, HMMRATAC), build a consensus peak set, count (featureCounts), test differential accessibility (DESeq2/edgeR, DiffBind, csaw). Downstream: motif enrichment (HOMER, MEME-ChIP), TF footprinting (TOBIAS, HINT-ATAC), and per-sample TF activity (chromVAR).

3D genome

Hi-C crosslinks chromatin, digests it, ligates nearby fragments and sequences the junctions, giving a genome-wide contact matrix. Micro-C uses MNase for nucleosome resolution; capture Hi-C, HiChIP and PLAC-seq focus on promoters or protein-anchored contacts. Processing (Juicer, HiC-Pro, distiller, HiCExplorer) produces .hic or .cool/.mcool files, normalised by iterative correction (ICE) or Knight–Ruiz balancing. Features are then called: A/B compartments (eigenvector of the correlation matrix), TADs (insulation score, Arrowhead, TopDom), loops (HiCCUPS, Mustache, chromosight), and differential contacts (diffHic, multiHiCcompare, CHESS). Contact frequency drops with genomic distance, so all comparisons must account for that distance decay.

Linking regulatory elements to genes

The nearest-gene assumption is wrong for a large share of enhancers. Better approaches use activity and contact together: the Activity-by-Contact (ABC) model (enhancer activity × Hi-C contact), correlations between accessibility and expression across samples or single cells (peak-to-gene links in ArchR, Signac), and CRISPRi screens as ground truth (e.g. ENCODE-rE2G models trained on CRISPR perturbation data).

RNA layerChapter 8

Transcriptomics

Measuring which genes are expressed, how much, and in which isoform. Bulk RNA-seq is the most widely used omics assay in biology, and its statistical tooling is the most mature, so it is the best place to learn the logic that every other layer reuses.

Research questions

  • Which genes change between conditions (disease vs control, treated vs untreated, time points)?
  • Which pathways and programmes are active, and which upstream regulators explain them?
  • Which isoforms, fusions, editing sites or allele-specific expression patterns are present?
  • Can expression classify patients (subtypes, prognosis, treatment response)?
  • How do genetic variants affect expression (eQTLs) and splicing (sQTLs)?

Experimental choices that decide what you can analyse

RNA selection

Poly(A)+ captures mature mRNA; rRNA depletion keeps non-polyadenylated and pre-mRNA (intronic reads). Small-RNA libraries for miRNA. Degraded samples (FFPE) need depletion or capture.

Strandedness and length

Stranded libraries (dUTP) resolve antisense transcription. Paired-end 2×100–150 bp helps isoforms and fusions. 3′-tag methods (QuantSeq, BRB-seq) are cheap for gene counts but blind to isoforms.

Depth and replicates

~20–30M reads per sample is typical for gene-level DE; isoform work needs more. Biological replicates matter more than depth: aim for six or more per group when you can.

The standard pipeline

  1. QC: FastQC/MultiQC, rRNA content, strand check (RSeQC infer_experiment, salmon's automatic library type), gene body coverage (3′ bias indicates degradation), RIN or DV200 from the lab.
  2. Quantification, two routes:
    • Alignment-based: STAR or HISAT2 to the genome, then featureCounts or HTSeq for gene counts; RSEM for isoforms. Gives BAMs for visual inspection, novel junctions and variant calling.
    • Pseudoalignment / selective alignment: salmon or kallisto match reads to transcript sequences and resolve multi-mapping reads with an EM algorithm. Fast and accurate for annotated transcripts; use salmon with a decoy-aware index. Import with tximport/tximeta to gene level.
  3. Filtering and normalisation: remove genes with very low counts (edgeR filterByExpr). Between-sample normalisation with DESeq2's median-of-ratios or edgeR's TMM. TPM and FPKM are within-sample units for display; do not feed them into count-based DE models.
  4. Exploration: variance-stabilising transform (vst/rlog), PCA, sample-distance heatmaps. Look for outliers, batch effects, sex mismatches (XIST, chrY genes) and sample swaps.
  5. Differential expression: negative binomial GLMs (DESeq2, edgeR) or linear models on log-CPM with precision weights (limma-voom). Include batch and covariates in the design matrix. Use empirical Bayes shrinkage of dispersions and of fold changes (apeglm, ashr) for ranking.
  6. Multiple testing: Benjamini–Hochberg FDR. IHW (independent hypothesis weighting) can increase power.
  7. Interpretation: over-representation (clusterProfiler, g:Profiler, Enrichr), rank-based GSEA (fgsea, GSEA), sample-level scores (GSVA, ssGSEA, singscore), TF and pathway activity from footprints (DoRothEA/CollecTRI with decoupleR, PROGENy), co-expression modules (WGCNA, hdWGCNA for single-cell).
Why the negative binomial

Counts from technical replicates are roughly Poisson (variance = mean). Biological replicates vary more, so variance = μ + αμ², where α is the dispersion. With three replicates you cannot estimate α per gene reliably, so DESeq2 and edgeR borrow information across genes: they shrink each gene's dispersion toward a trend fitted against mean expression. That shrinkage is the main reason these tools work with small sample sizes.

Beyond gene counts

  • Isoforms: short-read isoform quantification is uncertain when isoforms share most exons. Long-read RNA-seq (PacBio Kinnex/Iso-Seq, ONT cDNA or direct RNA) sequences full transcripts. Tools: IsoQuant, Bambu, FLAIR, StringTie2, TALON, ESPRESSO; SQANTI3 classifies and filters isoforms against the annotation.
  • Splicing: see the splicing chapter (rMATS, LeafCutter, MAJIQ, SUPPA2).
  • Fusion genes: STAR-Fusion, Arriba, FusionCatcher; important in leukaemia (BCR::ABL1), sarcomas and prostate cancer (TMPRSS2::ERG).
  • Allele-specific expression: phASER, GATK ASEReadCounter; WASP-style filtering corrects reference mapping bias.
  • RNA editing: REDItools, SPRINT, JACUSA2.
  • Small RNA: miRDeep2, sRNAbench, nf-core/smrnaseq; miRNA target prediction with TargetScan and miRDB.
  • Deconvolution: estimate cell-type proportions from bulk with CIBERSORTx, MuSiC, BayesPrism, EPIC, quanTIseq, using single-cell references.
  • Nascent and decay: PRO-seq, TT-seq and metabolic labelling (SLAM-seq) separate transcription from degradation.

  • Treating "not significant" as "not changed"; with low power, absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
Research gap
  • Accurate isoform-level quantification and differential testing, especially from long reads at modest depth.
  • Calibrated DE in large heterogeneous cohorts, where standard parametric tools can inflate false positives.
  • Separating transcriptional from post-transcriptional regulation in standard steady-state data.
  • Robust deconvolution when reference single-cell data come from a different platform or disease state.
RNA layer · resolutionChapter 9

Single-cell and spatial omics

Bulk profiles average thousands of cells. Single-cell methods measure each cell separately, revealing cell types, rare populations, continuous trajectories and cell-to-cell communication. Spatial methods add where each cell sits in the tissue.

Research questions

  • What cell types and states exist in a tissue, and which are new?
  • How do cell composition and cell-type-specific expression change in disease?
  • What are the developmental or differentiation trajectories, and what drives fate decisions?
  • Which cells communicate, through which ligand–receptor pairs, and in which tissue niches?
  • Which regulatory programmes (TFs, enhancers) define each state?
  • How does a genetic variant or perturbation act in specific cell types (single-cell eQTLs, Perturb-seq)?

The scRNA-seq workflow

  1. Pre-processing: demultiplex cell barcodes and UMIs, align and count (Cell Ranger, STARsolo, alevin-fry, kallisto|bustools). Output: a sparse cells × genes count matrix (h5ad, h5, mtx).
  2. Cell QC: total UMIs, genes detected, mitochondrial fraction (dying cells), ribosomal fraction. Prefer data-driven thresholds (median absolute deviations) per sample over fixed cut-offs. Remove ambient RNA (SoupX, CellBender, DecontX) and doublets (scDblFinder, DoubletFinder, scrublet). Sample multiplexing by genotype (demuxlet, souporcell, vireo) or hashtags (HTO) also identifies doublets.
  3. Normalisation: size-factor scaling with log1p (shifted logarithm), scran pooling, sctransform or analytic Pearson residuals.
  4. Feature selection: 2,000–5,000 highly variable genes; deviance-based selection is an alternative.
  5. Dimensionality reduction: PCA (or a VAE latent space such as scVI), then a k-nearest-neighbour graph. UMAP and t-SNE are for visualisation only; distances and cluster sizes in them are not meaningful.
  6. Integration / batch correction across samples: Harmony, scVI/scANVI, Seurat CCA/RPCA, Scanorama, BBKNN, fastMNN, LIGER. Over-correction can erase real biology (e.g. disease-specific states).
  7. Clustering: Leiden or Louvain community detection on the kNN graph; check robustness across resolutions.
  8. Annotation: marker genes plus automated label transfer (CellTypist, SingleR, Azimuth, scANVI, scArches) to reference atlases (Human Cell Atlas, Tabula Sapiens, CELLxGENE Census).
  9. Downstream: compositional analysis (scCODA, propeller, milo for neighbourhoods), differential expression by pseudobulk per sample and cell type, trajectories, velocity, communication, gene-regulatory networks.
The unit of replication is the donor, not the cell

Ten thousand cells from two patients are two biological replicates. Single-cell DE tests that treat cells as independent produce floods of false positives. Aggregate counts per sample and cell type (pseudobulk) and use DESeq2, edgeR or limma, or use mixed models with a donor random effect. This is the most important statistical lesson of the field.

Trajectories, velocity and fate

Pseudotime orders cells along a continuous process (Monocle 3, Slingshot, PAGA, Palantir, diffusion pseudotime). RNA velocity uses the ratio of unspliced to spliced reads to estimate the direction of change (velocyto, scVelo, with newer models such as veloVI, UniTVelo and dynamo); its assumptions (constant rates, steady-state kinetics) often fail, so treat velocity arrows as hypotheses. CellRank combines velocity, pseudotime or real time points into fate probabilities. Lineage tracing with heritable barcodes (CRISPR scars, LARRY, mitochondrial variants) provides ground truth that computational trajectories cannot.

Communication and regulation

Ligand–receptor inference (CellPhoneDB, CellChat, NicheNet, LIANA+) scores co-expression of ligand in sender cells and receptor in receiver cells using curated databases. These are hypotheses: mRNA co-expression does not prove protein signalling, and results depend heavily on the resource used. Gene-regulatory network inference (SCENIC/pySCENIC: GRNBoost2 co-expression + cis-regulatory motif pruning; CellOracle; SCENIC+ with multiome data) identifies regulons and TF activity.

Spatial analysis

  • Segmentation of imaging data into cells: Cellpose, Baysor, proseg, StarDist; errors here propagate into every downstream result.
  • Deconvolution of multi-cell spots using single-cell references: cell2location, RCTD (spacexr), CARD, Tangram, DestVI, SPOTlight.
  • Spatially variable genes: SpatialDE, SPARK-X, nnSVG, Moran's I in Squidpy.
  • Spatial domains / niches: BayesSpace, SpaGCN, GraphST, STAGATE, BANKSY, CellCharter.
  • Neighbourhood and interaction: co-occurrence and neighbourhood enrichment (Squidpy), spatially constrained ligand–receptor scoring (COMMOT, LIANA+), niche-aware DE.
  • Frameworks: Squidpy and SpatialData (Python, scverse), Giotto Suite, Seurat v5, Voyager, SpatialExperiment (Bioconductor).

Foundation models

Transformer models pre-trained on tens of millions of cells (Geneformer, scGPT, scFoundation, UCE, Nicheformer, and others) promise general-purpose embeddings for annotation, batch integration and perturbation prediction. Independent evaluations have been sobering: in zero-shot settings their embeddings often do not beat highly variable genes with PCA, scVI or Harmony, and for predicting perturbation effects deep models have not clearly outperformed simple linear or mean baselines. Fine-tuning helps for some tasks. Evaluate any claim against simple baselines on held-out data that the model did not see during pre-training.

Protein layerChapter 10

Proteomics

Proteins do most of the work in a cell and are the targets of most drugs. Proteomics identifies and quantifies them, their modifications and their interactions. There is no protein equivalent of PCR, so the field relies on mass spectrometry and affinity reagents, each with its own statistics.

Research questions

  • Which proteins and proteoforms differ between conditions, and do they follow mRNA changes?
  • Which signalling pathways are active (phosphoproteomics) and which kinases drive them?
  • Which plasma proteins are biomarkers or drug targets, and which are causal (pQTL + Mendelian randomisation)?
  • Which proteins form complexes, where are they located, and how do structures explain variant effects?
  • Which drug binds which protein (chemoproteomics, thermal proteome profiling)?

How bottom-up mass spectrometry works

  1. Sample preparation: lyse, reduce and alkylate cysteines, digest with trypsin (cuts after K/R) into peptides of ~7–30 residues. Optional enrichment for PTMs (phospho: TiO2, Fe-IMAC; ubiquitin: K-ε-GG antibody) or depletion of abundant plasma proteins.
  2. Liquid chromatography separates peptides by hydrophobicity over 10–120 minutes; they are ionised by electrospray.
  3. MS1 measures mass-to-charge (m/z) of intact peptide ions; MS2 fragments selected ions (CID/HCD, ETD) and measures fragment masses (b- and y-ions), which encode the sequence.
  4. Acquisition: DDA (data-dependent; picks the top-N most intense ions, giving stochastic missing values), DIA (data-independent; fragments all ions in m/z windows, more complete and reproducible, more complex spectra), targeted (SRM/PRM; predefined peptides, best for validation).
  5. Identification: match spectra against theoretical spectra from a protein database (MSFragger, Comet, Andromeda, Sage, Mascot) or spectral libraries (often predicted by deep learning: Prosit, AlphaPeptDeep, MS²PIP). Control FDR with target–decoy competition at peptide-spectrum match, peptide and protein levels (typically 1%); rescoring with Percolator or MS²Rescore.
  6. Protein inference: peptides shared between proteins create ambiguity, resolved into protein groups by parsimony.
  7. Quantification: label-free (MS1 intensity, MaxLFQ), isobaric tags (TMT/TMTpro: up to 18 samples multiplexed, measured at reporter ions; ratio compression from co-isolation), SILAC (metabolic labelling), or DIA fragment intensities.

Top-down proteomics

Measures intact proteins, so proteoforms with combinations of PTMs are preserved. Harder and lower throughput (ProSight, TopPIC).

Affinity proteomics

Olink (proximity extension assay; DNA-barcoded antibody pairs read by sequencing; ~5,400 proteins in Explore HT) and SomaScan (modified aptamers; ~11,000 assays). Scalable to biobanks (UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project). Platforms agree only moderately for many proteins, and epitope effects can create artefactual pQTLs.

Single-cell and spatial

SCoPE2, plexDIA and Astral-based single-cell proteomics quantify ~1,000–5,000 proteins per cell. Deep Visual Proteomics combines imaging, laser microdissection and MS. CODEX/PhenoCycler and IMC image ~40–100 proteins in tissue.

Statistical analysis

MS proteomics data have features that RNA-seq tools do not handle:

  • Missing values that are not random: low-abundance peptides are missing because they fell below detection (MNAR), while others are missing by chance (MCAR). Imputation choice changes results (minimum-value, left-censored QRILC or MinProb for MNAR; kNN or random forest for MCAR). Models that handle missingness directly (MSstats, proDA, limpa) are preferable to blind imputation.
  • Log-normal intensities: analyse log2 intensities with linear models (limma, MSstats, MSstatsTMT, DEqMS which accounts for the number of peptides per protein).
  • Normalisation: median centring, variance-stabilising normalisation (vsn), TMT reference channels (IRS) for multi-batch designs.
  • PTM site localisation probabilities (ptmRS, PTMProphet), and normalising PTM changes to protein abundance.
  • Kinase activity inference from phosphosites: KSEA, PTM-SEA, the Kinase Library (Johnson et al. 2023).

Structure and interactions

AlphaFold2 (2021) predicts single-chain structures with near-experimental accuracy for many proteins; the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database holds over 200 million predictions. AlphaFold3 (2024) and open alternatives (Boltz-1/2, Chai-1, RoseTTAFold All-Atom) predict complexes with nucleic acids, ligands and modifications. ESMFold predicts structure from a protein language model without multiple-sequence alignments. pLDDT and PAE confidence scores must be read before trusting a model: low pLDDT often marks intrinsically disordered regions. Interaction resources include STRING (integrated scores), BioGRID and IntAct (curated), BioPlex and HuRI (systematic AP-MS and Y2H maps), and CORUM (complexes).

Protein FDR is not peptide FDR. With large datasets, a 1% peptide-level FDR can produce a much higher protein-level FDR if not controlled separately. Report which levels were controlled.   • Principled missing-value modelling and uncertainty propagation from spectra to proteins.

Small-molecule layerChapter 11

Metabolomics and lipidomics

Metabolites are the substrates, products and signals of biochemistry: sugars, amino acids, nucleotides, lipids, cofactors, drugs and microbial products. They change within seconds and integrate genes, environment, diet and microbiome, which makes them the omics layer closest to phenotype.

Research questions

  • Which metabolites or pathways distinguish disease, and can they be biomarkers?
  • How do genes (mQTLs), diet, drugs and gut microbes shape circulating metabolites?
  • Where does carbon flow through central metabolism in a tumour or immune cell (isotope tracing)?
  • What is this unknown feature? (structure elucidation is the bottleneck of the field)
  • What has a person been exposed to (exposome)?

  1. Design: randomised injection order, pooled QC samples every 5–10 injections, blanks, internal standards, and long-term reference material (NIST SRM 1950 plasma).
  2. Conversion: vendor files to open formats (mzML) with ProteoWizard msconvert.

  4. Alignment and grouping across samples (retention-time correction), gap filling.
  5. Annotation of feature redundancy: group adducts ([M+H]+, [M+Na]+), isotopes and fragments of the same compound (CAMERA, MS-FLO, IPA).

  9. Pathway analysis: over-representation on KEGG, SMPDB or Reactome (MetaboAnalyst), or annotation-free mummichog / PIUMet that work directly from m/z values.
Identification confidence (Metabolomics Standards Initiative)

Level 1: confirmed with an authentic standard (same RT and MS/MS on the same system). Level 2: probable structure from library spectrum match. Level 3: tentative compound class. Level 4: unknown. Schymanski et al. (2014) added finer levels for high-resolution MS. Always report the level. Most untargeted features remain at level 3–4.

Lipidomics, flux and models

Lipidomics uses shorthand nomenclature (LIPID MAPS; e.g. PC 34:1 vs PC 16:0_18:1) whose level should match the evidence. Tools: LipidSearch, MS-DIAL, LipidXplorer, Lipid Data Analyzer, LipidMatch. Stable-isotope tracing follows 13C-glucose or 13C-glutamine into downstream metabolites; isotopologue distributions are corrected for natural abundance (IsoCor, AccuCor) and fluxes estimated with INCA or 13CFLUX2. Genome-scale metabolic models (Recon3D, Human1/Human-GEM, AGORA2 for gut microbes) predict fluxes by flux balance analysis under constraints (COBRApy, COBRA Toolbox, RAVEN); omics data constrain them to tissue or patient-specific models (GIMME, iMAT, tINIT).

Community layerChapter 12

Metagenomics and the microbiome

Humans carry roughly as many bacterial cells as human cells, and the gut microbiome encodes far more genes than our own genome. Metagenomics sequences the whole community to ask who is there, what they can do, and how they interact with the host.

Research questions

  • How does community composition differ in disease, and is it cause or consequence?
  • Which functions (pathways, antibiotic resistance genes, virulence factors) are present and expressed?
  • Which strains are shared between mother and infant, or transmitted between people?
  • Which microbial metabolites (short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, TMAO) mediate effects on the host?
  • Which genomes have never been cultured (metagenome-assembled genomes)?

Approaches

Amplicon (16S rRNA, ITS, 18S)

PCR of a marker gene's variable regions (e.g. V4). Cheap and sensitive; genus-level (sometimes species) taxonomy; no direct function. Denoise into exact amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) with DADA2, Deblur or UNOISE rather than 97% OTUs. Classify with SILVA, GTDB or Greengenes2. Infer function with PICRUSt2 (predicted, so interpret with care).

Shotgun metagenomics

Sequences all DNA. Taxonomic profiling by marker genes (MetaPhlAn 4) or k-mers (Kraken 2 + Bracken, sylph); functional profiling (HUMAnN 3); assembly (MEGAHIT, metaSPAdes, metaFlye), binning into MAGs (MetaBAT 2, SemiBin2, VAMB, DAS Tool), quality with CheckM2 (MIMAG completeness and contamination standards), taxonomy with GTDB-Tk; strain tracking (StrainPhlAn, inStrain).

Meta-transcriptomics, -proteomics, -metabolomics

Which genes are expressed (HUMAnN on RNA), which proteins are made, and which metabolites are present. Integrating these with the metagenome separates potential function from realised activity.

The statistics of compositions

Microbiome counts are compositional (only relative abundances are observed), sparse (many zeros), and overdispersed. Rarefying throws away data; total-sum scaling ignores compositionality. Use compositionally aware methods: centred or additive log-ratio transforms (CLR, ALR), ANCOM-BC2, ALDEx2, LinDA, or regression frameworks such as MaAsLin 2/3 with careful normalisation. Diversity: alpha (richness, Shannon, Faith's PD) and beta (Bray–Curtis, UniFrac, Aitchison distance) tested with PERMANOVA (vegan adonis2), checking dispersion first (betadisper).

Pitfalls
  • Contamination dominates low-biomass samples (tissue, blood, placenta). Include extraction and PCR blanks and use decontam.
  • Host DNA can be over 90% of reads in tissue; deplete or sequence deeper.
  • Medication (metformin, proton-pump inhibitors, antibiotics), diet and transit time confound case–control differences.
  • Different differential-abundance methods give different answers on the same data; report results from several methods or a consensus.

Research gap
  • Moving from association to mechanism: causal inference, gnotobiotic validation, metabolite mediation.
  • Strain-level functional differences and horizontal gene transfer tracking.
  • Viromes and phages, the least annotated part of the microbiome.

Specialised layersChapter 13

More omics worth knowing

Specialised layers that sit between the main ones. Each answers a question the big four cannot, and several are fertile ground for methods development because their tooling is younger.

Epitranscriptomics

More than 170 RNA modifications are known. m6A (N6-methyladenosine), written by METTL3/METTL14, read by YTHDF/YTHDC proteins and erased by FTO/ALKBH5, affects mRNA stability, splicing and translation. Mapping: antibody-based MeRIP-seq/m6A-seq (peaks, ~100–200 nt resolution) and miCLIP (single-nucleotide), antibody-free GLORI and eTAM-seq (quantitative stoichiometry), and direct RNA nanopore sequencing, where modifications alter the current signal (m6Anet, CHEUI, xPore, Dorado modification models). Pseudouridine (BID-seq), m5C (bisulfite on RNA) and A-to-I editing are the other common targets. Gap: quantitative, isoform-resolved maps and validation of nanopore-based calls.

Translatomics

Ribo-seq sequences ribosome-protected fragments (~28–30 nt) and shows where ribosomes are, with 3-nt periodicity revealing the reading frame. Uses: translation efficiency (Ribo-seq / RNA-seq; anota2seq, Xtail, riborex, deltaTE), discovery of upstream and small ORFs and micropeptides (RiboCode, ORFquant, PRICE, RiboTISH, Ribo-TISH), and ribosome stalling. QC with RiboseQC and riboWaltz. GENCODE now catalogues Ribo-seq ORFs (Mudge et al. 2022).

Immunomics and immune repertoires

T- and B-cell receptors are generated by V(D)J recombination, producing ~1015 possible receptors. AIRR-seq sequences these from bulk or single cells (10x VDJ). Tools: MiXCR, IgBLAST, TRUST4 (from RNA-seq), Immcantation (change-O, SHazaM for somatic hypermutation, clonal lineages), scirpy (single-cell, scverse). Questions: clonal expansion, convergent responses to vaccines and infection, antigen specificity prediction (TCRdist, GLIPH2, and deep models with modest generalisation to unseen epitopes). Immunopeptidomics measures HLA-presented peptides by MS to train neoantigen predictors (NetMHCpan, MHCflurry).

Glycomics and glycoproteomics

Glycans are branched, non-templated structures attached to proteins and lipids. They control protein folding, cell recognition, immune function (IgG Fc glycosylation tunes antibody effector activity) and are altered in cancer. Analysis: released-glycan MS or HPLC, intact glycopeptide MS (pGlyco, MSFragger-Glyco, Byonic), databases GlyTouCan and GlyGen. Gap: structural isomers are hard to distinguish, and glycan biosynthesis is only indirectly coded in the genome.

Cell-free DNA, liquid biopsy and fragmentomics

Dying cells release DNA fragments into blood, with a ~167 bp peak reflecting nucleosome protection. Tumour-derived cfDNA (ctDNA) can be detected through mutations (deep targeted sequencing with UMIs), copy number (low-pass WGS; ichorCNA estimates tumour fraction), methylation patterns that also reveal tissue of origin (multi-cancer early detection tests), and fragment size, end motifs and coverage at TSSs (DELFI and related fragmentomics methods). Non-invasive prenatal testing uses the same principles. Gap: sensitivity for early-stage cancers, where ctDNA fractions can be below 0.1%.

Interactomics and functional genomics screens

Pooled CRISPR screens (knockout, CRISPRi, CRISPRa, base and prime editing) measure fitness or reporter effects for every gene or variant; analyse with MAGeCK, BAGEL2, CRISPResso2 (editing outcomes) and the DepMap resources (Chronos-corrected gene effects across ~1,000 cancer cell lines). Perturb-seq combines CRISPR perturbations with single-cell RNA readouts; genome-scale Perturb-seq (Replogle et al. 2022) maps transcriptional consequences of nearly every expressed gene. MPRAs (massively parallel reporter assays) test thousands of regulatory variants for activity at once.

Phenomics, imaging and clinical omics

  • Cell Painting: multiplexed fluorescent stains and CellProfiler or deep features produce morphological profiles for drug and gene perturbations (JUMP-Cell Painting consortium).
  • Pathomics: deep learning on whole-slide H&E images predicts mutations, expression and survival; foundation models (UNI, CONCH, Virchow, Prov-GigaPath) provide general-purpose embeddings.
  • Radiogenomics: linking imaging features to molecular subtypes.
  • EHR phenomics: phenotype definitions (phecodes, HPO, OMOP common data model) enable PheWAS and biobank-scale genetics.

Exposomics

The exposome is the totality of environmental exposures over a lifetime: chemicals, diet, pollutants, infections, social factors. High-resolution MS of blood and urine detects thousands of exogenous chemicals; ExWAS (exposome-wide association studies) parallel GWAS. Gaps: chemical identification (even darker than the endogenous metabolome), time-varying exposure, and correlated exposures that confound each other.

Why layers disagree

A frequent surprise is that layers measured on the same samples correlate less than expected. That disagreement is usually biology, not a failed experiment:

  • Different timescales. Metabolites turn over in seconds, mRNAs in minutes to hours, proteins in hours to weeks. After a stimulus, mRNA peaks and falls before protein rises. Snapshot data at one time point capture different phases.
  • Post-transcriptional regulation. Translation efficiency, microRNAs, RNA-binding proteins and protein degradation (ubiquitin–proteasome, autophagy) all intervene between mRNA and protein.
  • Buffering and dosage compensation. Subunits of complexes are degraded when produced in excess, so an extra chromosome copy (aneuploidy) raises mRNA more than protein for complex members.
  • Enzyme kinetics. Flux depends on substrate concentration relative to Km, on allosteric control and on cofactors. A doubled enzyme often does not double flux.
  • Compartments and cell types. Bulk tissue mixes cells; a metabolite may come from a different cell type, or from microbes, than the enzyme you measured.
  • Technical noise and detection limits. Each platform measures a different subset with different dynamic range and missingness.
Evidence

Reviews of mRNA–protein relationships conclude that mRNA levels explain a substantial but incomplete part of protein variation across genes, and much less across samples for the same gene, with translation rates and protein turnover accounting for much of the rest (Liu, Beyer & Aebersold 2016, Cell; Buccitelli & Selbach 2020, Nature Reviews Genetics). In tumour cohorts (CPTAC), mRNA–protein correlation per gene across samples has a median around 0.4–0.5, lowest for stable housekeeping and complex proteins.

The QTL framework: genetics as the anchor

Genotype is fixed at conception and is not changed by disease, so associations from genotype to a molecular trait have a known causal direction. This makes molecular QTL mapping the backbone of most multi-omics interpretation:

  • Mapping: linear regression of normalised molecular phenotype on genotype dosage with covariates (genotype PCs, hidden factors from PEER or PCA). Tools: tensorQTL (GPU), QTLtools, FastQTL, MatrixEQTL; single-cell: pseudobulk per cell type or SAIGE-QTL, jaxQTL.
  • Resources: GTEx (49 tissues, eQTL and sQTL), eQTLGen (blood eQTLs, ~31,000 samples), the eQTL Catalogue (uniformly processed studies), OneK1K (single-cell blood eQTLs), UK Biobank PPP and deCODE (pQTLs), and the GoDMC (methylation QTLs).

From association to causation

Colocalisation

Do the GWAS and QTL signals share one causal variant, or are they two distinct variants in LD? coloc (Bayesian; posterior H4 for shared variant), coloc-SuSiE for multiple signals, eCAVIAR, HyPrColoc for many traits at once.

TWAS / PWAS

Predict gene expression (or protein) from genotype in a reference panel, then associate predicted expression with the trait in GWAS summary statistics (PrediXcan/S-PrediXcan, FUSION, UTMOST). Fast, but hits can reflect LD and co-regulation, not causal genes.

Mendelian randomisation

Use variants as instruments for an exposure (e.g. a cis-pQTL for a protein) to estimate its causal effect on an outcome. Assumes relevance, independence and no horizontal pleiotropy. Tools: TwoSampleMR, MendelianRandomization, MR-PRESSO, SMR with HEIDI test. Cis-pQTL MR is now used to prioritise drug targets.

Example: PCSK9 across the layers

Rare loss-of-function variants in PCSK9 lower LDL cholesterol and coronary disease risk (genome → phenome). PCSK9 protein binds the LDL receptor and targets it for degradation (proteome), so less PCSK9 means more receptors and lower plasma LDL (metabolome/lipidome). The genetics predicted that inhibiting PCSK9 would be safe and effective, and antibody (evolocumab, alirocumab) and siRNA (inclisiran) drugs followed. Drug programmes with human genetic support are roughly two to three times more likely to reach approval (Minikel et al. 2024, Nature).

Multi-omics · methodsChapter 15

Integrating multi-omics data

Integration means analysing two or more layers jointly so that the result is more than the sum of separate analyses. The right method depends on the question, on whether layers come from the same samples or cells, and on how much prior knowledge you want to impose.

When to combine: early, intermediate, late

Sample structure determines what is possible

Matched (vertical)

All layers measured on the same samples or cells. Allows true joint models (MOFA+, DIABLO, WNN, totalVI). The anchor is the sample.

Unmatched (diagonal)

Different samples or cells per layer, same biology. Needs feature-level links (gene activity from ATAC, prior graphs): GLUE, LIGER/UINMF, Seurat bridge integration. Riskier assumptions.

Mosaic

Some samples have some layers. Methods: MultiVI, StabMap, MIDAS, Cobolt. Common in real consortia data.

Horizontal

Same layer across batches or studies. This is batch integration (Harmony, scVI), not multi-omics, but usually comes first.

Core method families explained

Matrix factorisation and latent factor models

Each layer Xm (samples × features) is approximated as Z·WmT, where Z (samples × K factors) is shared and Wm holds layer-specific weights. MOFA/MOFA+ is a Bayesian group factor analysis with sparsity priors and per-layer likelihoods (Gaussian, Bernoulli, Poisson); it reports variance explained per factor per layer, so you see which factors are shared and which are private. JIVE separates joint from individual structure explicitly. MCIA and RGCCA maximise covariance between layer-specific projections. intNMF uses non-negative factorisation suited to clustering. iCluster/iClusterBayes fit a joint latent variable model for subtype discovery.

Similarity and network fusion

SNF builds a patient-similarity network per layer and iteratively fuses them through message passing until they converge; spectral clustering of the fused network gives subtypes. Robust to different scales and noise, easy to apply, but feature-level interpretation is indirect. NEMO extends this to partial data.

Supervised multi-block methods

DIABLO (mixOmics) finds sparse components that are correlated across layers and discriminate a categorical outcome, controlled by a design matrix of how strongly layers should correlate. Good for biomarker panels with cross-validation. Multi-block sPLS and penalised regression with layer-specific penalties (priority-Lasso, IPF-Lasso) are alternatives.

Deep generative models

Variational autoencoders with modality-specific encoders and decoders learn a joint latent space while modelling count noise properly: totalVI (RNA + surface proteins), MultiVI (RNA + ATAC, with mosaic support), PeakVI, GLUE (uses a prior regulatory graph to link unpaired modalities), scMM, MIDAS. Bulk-level examples include multi-omics autoencoders for survival (e.g. in TCGA). Graph neural networks operate on biological networks with omics features as node attributes. These models are flexible but need more data and careful evaluation against simple baselines.

Knowledge-driven integration

Instead of letting the data discover structure, map all layers onto prior networks: pathways (Reactome, KEGG), signalling (OmniPath), metabolic models (Human-GEM), regulatory networks (CollecTRI). COSMOS links phosphoproteomics, transcriptomics and metabolomics through a causal prior knowledge network using integer linear programming. Omics Integrator solves a prize-collecting Steiner forest to connect measured hits through the interactome. ActivePathways merges p-values across layers per pathway. These are more interpretable but limited by the completeness and bias of the prior knowledge.

A practical recipe

  1. Write down the question and the relationship you expect (previous chapter). Pick the method family from the task table.
  2. Process each layer well on its own first: QC, normalisation, batch correction, and single-layer results. If a layer is uninformative alone, integration rarely rescues it.
  3. Harmonise samples and identifiers: one sample sheet with IDs per layer; check sample identity across layers (genotype concordance from RNA and DNA, sex markers) to detect swaps.
  4. Transform to comparable scales: variance-stabilised counts, log intensities, M-values, CLR for compositions. Scale features or weight layers so the largest layer does not dominate.
  5. Select features (highly variable per layer) to keep layers balanced and reduce noise.
  6. Handle missing data explicitly: methods that tolerate missing views (MOFA+, MultiVI, late fusion) are better than imputing whole layers.
  7. Fit, then inspect: variance explained per layer, factor–covariate associations (batch must not drive factors), stability across random seeds and subsamples.
  8. Interpret with feature weights, enrichment per factor, and links back to known biology.
  9. Validate on an independent cohort or with an orthogonal experiment. For prediction, nest feature selection inside cross-validation to avoid leakage.

The honest summary of the benchmarks

No integration method wins everywhere. Adding layers does not automatically improve subtyping or prediction; a well-processed single layer (often transcriptomics) can match multi-omics models. Gains are largest when layers carry complementary information and sample sizes are adequate. Simple baselines (concatenated PCA, late fusion of separately trained models) should always be reported.

Research gap
  • Benchmarks with real ground truth for multi-omics (spike-in designs, simulations calibrated to real data, perturbation experiments).
  • Integration that respects causal direction and time, rather than symmetric correlation.
  • Uncertainty quantification for latent factors and integrated predictions.
  • Scalable, privacy-preserving (federated) integration across cohorts that cannot share data.
  • Interpretable deep integration: tying latent dimensions back to pathways and mechanisms.
  • Mosaic integration with many missing layers, typical of clinical cohorts.
PracticeChapter 16

Study design, workflows and reproducibility

Most failed omics projects fail in design or data management, not in algorithms. This chapter covers the practical layer: design, batch effects, file formats, public data, workflow managers and reporting.

Design before data

  • Power: estimate from pilot or public data (RNA-seq: RNASeqPower, PROPER, ssizeRNA; scRNA-seq: scPower, hierarchicell; GWAS: genpwr). Biological replicates beat sequencing depth.
  • Randomise and block: spread conditions across extraction days, library batches, flow cells and MS runs. Record everything in sample metadata.
  • Controls: spike-ins (ERCC, SIRVs, sequins), pooled QC samples, reference materials (GIAB cell lines, Quartet multi-omics reference materials, NIST plasma), negative controls (blanks).
  • Matched multi-omics: split the same aliquot across assays where possible; time from collection to freezing matters most for RNA and metabolites.

Batch effects

If batch is not confounded with the variable of interest, include it as a covariate in the model (preferred) or correct it: ComBat (empirical Bayes on continuous data), ComBat-seq (counts), limma removeBatchEffect (for visualisation only), RUVSeq/RUVg (using control genes), SVA (surrogate variables for unknown batches), and for single-cell, Harmony or scVI. Check before and after with PCA coloured by batch and by biology, kBET or LISI scores, and silhouette widths.

Pitfall

Correcting batch and then testing for group differences with the corrected data can exaggerate significance, especially when the design is unbalanced (Nygaard et al. 2016, Biostatistics). Model batch in the test instead of pre-correcting when possible.

Benchmark hub

What independent, neutral comparisons of tools have found. Benchmarks age quickly and can be biased by their choice of data and metrics, so read them for patterns and for what they measured, not only for the winner.

How to read a benchmark

Who ran it?

Neutral benchmarks (by groups that did not develop any of the tools) are more trustworthy than the comparison table in a new tool's own paper, where the new tool almost always wins (the "self-assessment trap").

What is the ground truth?

Simulations (controllable, but may not match reality), spike-ins and mixtures (realistic, limited), reference materials (GIAB, Quartet), orthogonal validation, or consistency only (weakest).

Which metric?

Precision, recall, F1, AUROC, AUPRC, FDR calibration, runtime, memory. A tool can win on one and lose on another. Check whether the metric matches your use.

Does it match your data?

Species, platform, depth, sample size and tissue all change rankings. A method that wins on cell lines may not win on tumour biopsies.

Guidelines for running a good benchmark: Weber et al. 2019, Genome Biology. Continuous, community-maintained benchmarks (Open Problems in Single-Cell Analysis, OpenEBench, precisionFDA challenges, CAMI, CASP) are replacing one-off papers.

ResearchChapter 19

Research questions and open gaps

A map of what is unsolved, organised so you can find a thesis-sized problem. Each gap names why it is hard and what a contribution could look like. Strong PhD projects usually sit where a biological question, a data resource and a methodological weakness meet.

Cross-cutting gaps

1 · Causality, not correlation

Most multi-omics results are associations. Combining genetic anchors (QTLs, MR), perturbations (CRISPR, drugs) and time series to build causal multi-layer models is a central open problem.

Contribution: a causal integration method validated against Perturb-seq or CRISPR screens.

2 · Ground truth for evaluation

Many fields (spatial domains, GRNs, multi-omics integration, cell communication) lack ground truth, so methods cannot be compared fairly.

Contribution: a benchmark dataset or calibrated simulator, plus a neutral comparison.

3 · Context specificity

Variant and regulatory effects depend on cell type, state, stimulus and development. Current reference maps cover few contexts.

Contribution: dynamic or single-cell QTL mapping; models that transfer effects across contexts.

4 · Diversity and equity

Reference panels, GWAS, QTL resources and training data for predictors over-represent European ancestry, which limits accuracy elsewhere.

Contribution: multi-ancestry fine-mapping, PRS or QTL methods; analysis of under-represented cohorts.

5 · Foundation models, honestly evaluated

Large pre-trained models for DNA, RNA, proteins and cells are proliferating, but evaluations frequently show they do not beat simple baselines on key tasks.

Contribution: rigorous evaluation, failure analysis, or task-specific models that demonstrably add value.

6 · Uncertainty and calibration

Most pipelines output point estimates. Propagating uncertainty from raw signal to biological conclusion (quantification, imputation, latent factors) is rare.

Contribution: Bayesian or conformal methods that give calibrated intervals.

7 · Interpretability

Deep models predict well but do not explain. Linking latent dimensions to pathways and mechanisms remains hard.

Contribution: biologically constrained architectures (pathway-informed, sparse) with validation.

8 · Scale, privacy and federation

Biobanks with millions of participants and atlases with hundreds of millions of cells strain current algorithms; human data often cannot leave its institution.

Contribution: scalable or federated algorithms, sketching, out-of-core methods.

How to choose a PhD problem in this field

  1. Start from a biological question that someone cares about (a disease, a developmental process, a drug response), then ask which layer limits the answer.
  2. Secure data early: public cohorts (TCGA, GTEx, UK Biobank, CELLxGENE), a collaborator's matched multi-omics, or your own. Many methods projects stall for lack of the right dataset.
  3. Find the methodological weakness using the benchmark hub: where do existing tools disagree, fail on certain data, or lack evaluation?
  4. Plan a validation: an orthogonal experiment, an independent cohort, a held-out perturbation, or a simulation calibrated to real data.
  5. Scope three papers: commonly (a) a benchmark or resource, (b) a method, (c) a biological application of the method.
  6. Release software as a documented package (Bioconductor, PyPI/scverse ecosystem) with tests; it multiplies the impact of the thesis.
Ten concrete starting questions
  1. Do single-cell foundation model embeddings improve cell-type-specific eQTL mapping compared with pseudobulk on annotated clusters?
  2. Can long-read RNA-seq resolve which GWAS-colocalised sQTLs change protein-coding potential?
  3. Which share of GWAS loci without eQTL colocalisation are explained by caQTLs in disease-relevant cell types?
  4. How robust are multi-omics cancer subtypes (MOFA+, SNF, iCluster) across independent cohorts and platforms?
  5. Can cis-pQTL Mendelian randomisation and metabolite QTLs together identify enzyme targets with on-target metabolic biomarkers?
  6. Does integrating phosphoproteomics with transcriptomics (e.g. COSMOS) predict drug response better than either layer alone in cell-line panels?
  7. How much do spatial deconvolution results change with the choice of single-cell reference, and can this uncertainty be modelled?
  8. What fraction of untargeted metabolomic features in human plasma are microbial in origin, and can metagenomes predict them?
  9. Can calibrated variant effect predictors from protein language models reduce VUS rates in under-represented ancestries?
  10. How should a multi-omics study allocate a fixed budget between more samples and more layers?
