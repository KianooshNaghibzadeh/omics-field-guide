How the layers talk to each other

Before integrating data computationally, decide what biological relationship you expect between the layers. The relationship determines which features you can link, which direction effects flow, how strong correlations should be, and which method is appropriate.

Why layers disagree

A frequent surprise is that layers measured on the same samples correlate less than expected. That disagreement is usually biology, not a failed experiment:

  • Different timescales. Metabolites turn over in seconds, mRNAs in minutes to hours, proteins in hours to weeks. After a stimulus, mRNA peaks and falls before protein rises. Snapshot data at one time point capture different phases.
  • Post-transcriptional regulation. Translation efficiency, microRNAs, RNA-binding proteins and protein degradation (ubiquitin–proteasome, autophagy) all intervene between mRNA and protein.
  • Buffering and dosage compensation. Subunits of complexes are degraded when produced in excess, so an extra chromosome copy (aneuploidy) raises mRNA more than protein for complex members.
  • Enzyme kinetics. Flux depends on substrate concentration relative to Km, on allosteric control and on cofactors. A doubled enzyme often does not double flux.
  • Compartments and cell types. Bulk tissue mixes cells; a metabolite may come from a different cell type, or from microbes, than the enzyme you measured.
  • Technical noise and detection limits. Each platform measures a different subset with different dynamic range and missingness.
Evidence

Reviews of mRNA–protein relationships conclude that mRNA levels explain a substantial but incomplete part of protein variation across genes, and much less across samples for the same gene, with translation rates and protein turnover accounting for much of the rest (Liu, Beyer & Aebersold 2016, Cell; Buccitelli & Selbach 2020, Nature Reviews Genetics). In tumour cohorts (CPTAC), mRNA–protein correlation per gene across samples has a median around 0.4–0.5, lowest for stable housekeeping and complex proteins.

The QTL framework: genetics as the anchor

Genotype is fixed at conception and is not changed by disease, so associations from genotype to a molecular trait have a known causal direction. This makes molecular QTL mapping the backbone of most multi-omics interpretation:

  • Mapping: linear regression of normalised molecular phenotype on genotype dosage with covariates (genotype PCs, hidden factors from PEER or PCA). Tools: tensorQTL (GPU), QTLtools, FastQTL, MatrixEQTL; single-cell: pseudobulk per cell type or SAIGE-QTL, jaxQTL.
  • Resources: GTEx (49 tissues, eQTL and sQTL), eQTLGen (blood eQTLs, ~31,000 samples), the eQTL Catalogue (uniformly processed studies), OneK1K (single-cell blood eQTLs), UK Biobank PPP and deCODE (pQTLs), and the GoDMC (methylation QTLs).

From association to causation

Colocalisation

Do the GWAS and QTL signals share one causal variant, or are they two distinct variants in LD? coloc (Bayesian; posterior H4 for shared variant), coloc-SuSiE for multiple signals, eCAVIAR, HyPrColoc for many traits at once.

TWAS / PWAS

Predict gene expression (or protein) from genotype in a reference panel, then associate predicted expression with the trait in GWAS summary statistics (PrediXcan/S-PrediXcan, FUSION, UTMOST). Fast, but hits can reflect LD and co-regulation, not causal genes.

Mendelian randomisation

Use variants as instruments for an exposure (e.g. a cis-pQTL for a protein) to estimate its causal effect on an outcome. Assumes relevance, independence and no horizontal pleiotropy. Tools: TwoSampleMR, MendelianRandomization, MR-PRESSO, SMR with HEIDI test. Cis-pQTL MR is now used to prioritise drug targets.

Example: PCSK9 across the layers

Rare loss-of-function variants in PCSK9 lower LDL cholesterol and coronary disease risk (genome → phenome). PCSK9 protein binds the LDL receptor and targets it for degradation (proteome), so less PCSK9 means more receptors and lower plasma LDL (metabolome/lipidome). The genetics predicted that inhibiting PCSK9 would be safe and effective, and antibody (evolocumab, alirocumab) and siRNA (inclisiran) drugs followed. Drug programmes with human genetic support are roughly two to three times more likely to reach approval (Minikel et al. 2024, Nature).

Research gap
  • Many GWAS loci do not colocalise with any known eQTL ("missing regulation"); context-specific, single-cell and developmental QTLs may close the gap (Mostafavi et al. 2023, Nature Genetics).
  • Time-resolved multi-omics that captures lags between layers.
  • Causal models across more than two layers (variant → methylation → expression → protein → trait) with proper uncertainty.
  • Protein-to-metabolite links that account for flux, not just enzyme abundance.
